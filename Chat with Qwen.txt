# 🗺️ THE MASTER PIPELINE: Cross-Population Generalization in Cardiac AI
**Core Objective:** Prove whether ECG Foundation Models (Signal Encoders) fail on diverse populations due to a *Perception Error* (unfamiliar biology) or a *Reasoning Shortcut* (Prevalence-Proxy Bias), and surgically fix it without retraining.

---

## 🏗️ PHASE 0: FOUNDATION & THE SAFETY NET (Weeks 1-4)
*Goal: Secure data, build the harness, and lock in publishability before running a single experiment.*

*   **Step 0.1: Data Acquisition (100% Public, No Private Collaborations Needed)**
    *   **Tier 1 (The Anchor - Within-Site Ancestry):** Apply for **MIMIC-IV-ECG** (US hospital, race/ethnicity labels, holds acquisition constant) and **UK Biobank** (includes South Asian ancestry cohort).
    *   **Tier 2 (Cross-Site/Device):** Download open datasets (PTB-XL [Germany], CODE-15 [Brazil], Chapman [China]).
    *   **Tier 3 (The Global South Reality):** Download **ECG-Image-Kit** to simulate paper-vs-digital ECG artifacts.
*   **Step 0.2: Model Setup (The "Cameras")**
    *   Load open-weight Signal Encoders: **ECG-FM**, **ECGFounder**, and **CSFM**.
    *   *Crucial Check:* Verify we can extract internal mathematical embeddings (activations) from the middle and final layers.
*   **Step 0.3: The Pre-Registration (The Ultimate Shield)**
    *   Timestamp the hypothesis, the 5-way decomposition math, and the decision tree on OSF/AsPredicted.
    *   *Why?* This guarantees that **no matter what the data shows, it is a publishable scientific result**, not "fishing for a story."

## 🔬 PHASE 1: DECOMPOSE (The Autopsy of the Gap)
*Goal: Mathematically split the total error gap into 5 distinct causal suspects using Shapley Values.*

**The Experiment:** Compare model performance across ancestry groups *within the same hospital* (MIMIC/UKB) to isolate biology from machine errors.

*   **Suspect 1: Prevalence Shift** (Disease is just more common in Group A).
    *   *Test:* Mathematically reweight the target group's labels to match the source group.
*   **Suspect 2: Acquisition Shift** (Different machines, paper vs. digital).
    *   *Test:* Use ECG-Image-Kit to render digital ECGs as "paper photos" and measure the error spike.
*   **Suspect 3: Benign Morphology** (Harmless genetic variations misread as disease).
    *   *Test:* Evaluate False Positive Rate *only* on gold-standard confirmed healthy patients.
*   **Suspect 4: True Pathology** (The disease genuinely looks physically different).
    *   *Test:* Evaluate Sensitivity *only* on gold-standard confirmed sick patients.
*   **Suspect 5: Labeling Shift** (Different hospitals use different thresholds for "abnormal").
    *   *Test:* Harmonize all labels to one universal mathematical standard.

### 🌳 PHASE 1 DECISION TREE (Where do we go next?)
*   **➡️ Branch 1A (Artifact Dominates):** The gap is mostly machine/labeling errors. *Action:* Publish the harmonization fix. Proceed to Phase 2 on the residual biological gap.
*   **➡️ Branch 1B (Benign Morphology Dominates):** The model is flagging healthy variants as sick. *Action:* This sets up the Shortcut Hypothesis. Proceed to Phase 2. *(Expected Path)*
*   **➡️ Branch 1C (True Pathology Dominates):** The disease actually looks different. *Action:* Publish the "Atlas of Population-Specific ECG Manifestation."
*   **➡️ Branch 1D (Gap is Small):** Modern models are actually robust. *Action:* Publish a contrarian audit overturning the field's alarmist narrative.

## 🧠 PHASE 2: LOCALIZE (The Brain Scan)
*Goal: Open the Signal Encoder's "brain" using Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) to find the physical "Shortcut Wire."*

**The Experiment:** We are looking for a mathematical direction inside the AI's embedding space that represents `[Demographic Fingerprint] ➔ [High Disease Prior]`.

*   **Step 2.1: Train SAEs** on the internal activations of ECG-FM to untangle the messy math into human-readable "features" (wires).
*   **Step 2.2: Probing**
    *   Train a linear probe to predict Ancestry from the features.
    *   Train a linear probe to predict Disease Risk from the features.
*   **Step 2.3: The Smoking Gun (Activation Patching / Causal Mediation)**
    *   Take a healthy White patient's ECG.
    *   *Inject* the "South Asian Demographic Feature" from a different ECG into the AI's brain while it reads the White patient's heart.
    *   *Observe:* Does the AI suddenly flag the healthy White patient as sick?

### 🌳 PHASE 2 DECISION TREE (What did we find?)
*   **➡️ Branch 2A (Clean Shortcut Found):** Injecting the demographic feature *causes* the false alarm. We have proven the Prevalence-Proxy Shortcut. *Action:* Proceed to Phase 3 (Surgery). **(FLAGSHIP DISCOVERY)**
*   **➡️ Branch 2B (Entangled/Wrecking Ball):** The demographic wire and the disease wire are mathematically fused. We cannot separate them. *Action:* Publish the "Pareto Frontier of Equity vs. Accuracy" (explaining *why* debiasing is hard).
*   **➡️ Branch 2C (No Shortcut / Pure OOD):** Patching does nothing. The AI genuinely just can't "see" the morphology. *Action:* Pivot to Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) adaptation techniques. Contradicts the imaging literature!
*   **➡️ Branch 2D (Different Driver):** We find a different shortcut (e.g., a machine-specific artifact wire). *Action:* Target that specific wire in Phase 3.

## ✂️ PHASE 3: SEVER (The Surgery)
*Goal: Snip the shortcut wire at Inference-Time using LEACE, requiring ZERO new data and ZERO retraining.*

**The Experiment:** Apply a mathematical projection (LEACE) to flatten the "Demographic Direction" to zero, while preserving the "Diagnostic Direction."

*   **Step 3.1: Linear Erasure (LEACE)**
    *   Calculate the vector representing the demographic shortcut.
    *   Project the incoming patient's ECG embedding into the "null space" of that vector (turning the volume knob on the bias to zero).
*   **Step 3.2: The Physiological-Preservation Constraint**
    *   Verify that the AI can still perfectly detect obvious diseases (e.g., clear STEMI, Atrial Fibrillation). We must not blind the AI to real pathology.
*   **Step 3.3: Head-to-Head Baselines**
    *   Compare our surgical fix against: Full Fine-Tuning, Adversarial Debiasing, and Reweighting.

### 🌳 PHASE 3 DECISION TREE (Did the surgery work?)
*   **➡️ Branch 3A (Fix Works, No Accuracy Loss):** We closed the equity gap without hurting overall accuracy. *Action:* **NATURE MEDICINE PAPER.** A field-changing, deployable method for the Global South.
*   **➡️ Branch 3B (Fix Works, but Costs Accuracy):** The Pareto Tradeoff. We fixed bias, but the model got slightly dumber overall because the traits were entangled. *Action:* Map the exact tradeoff curve. High-tier ML paper.
*   **➡️ Branch 3C (Inference-Time Fails, Needs Minimal Tuning):** LEACE isn't enough; we need to apply a tiny LoRA adapter to just the shortcut subspace. *Action:* Publish "Label-Efficient Targeted Correction."
*   **➡️ Branch 3D (Irreducible Without Data):** Nothing works without collecting local target data. *Action:* Publish a massive Negative Result redirecting the field away from post-hoc fixes.

## 1. ABSTRACT

Cardiac foundation models (e.g., ECG-FM, ECGFounder, CSFM) demonstrate robust performance on in-distribution data but suffer significant degradation and increased false-positive rates when deployed across diverse populations. Current mitigation strategies predominantly rely on data-centric approaches—such as retraining on diverse cohorts or adversarial debiasing—which are computationally prohibitive, require massive labeled datasets, and often fail to transfer across clinical sites. We propose a paradigm shift: treating cross-population failure not as a perceptual deficit (an inability to "see" unfamiliar morphology), but as a learned **representational shortcut**. 

We introduce a four-stage causal-mechanistic framework: (1) **Causal Decomposition** of the performance gap into five distinct drivers (prevalence, acquisition, benign morphology, true pathology, and labeling shifts) using Shapley-value attribution; (2) **Mechanistic Localization** using Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) to identify internal "demographic-proxy" circuits within the model’s latent space; (3) **Surgical Erasure** via inference-time linear projection (LEACE) to sever the shortcut without retraining or new labels; and (4) **Generalization** across three foundation models and multiple ancestry axes. Preliminary analysis of the 2025–2026 literature suggests that demographic signatures in ECGs are largely environmental rather than genetic, supporting the hypothesis that models exploit these signatures as prevalence proxies. This work aims to deliver the first label-free, inference-time correction for physiological foundation models, offering a deployable solution for global health equity that circumvents the "data arms race."

## 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

### 2.1 The Generalization Crisis in Cardiac AI
Recent advancements in self-supervised learning have produced powerful foundation models for electrocardiography. **ECGFounder** (NEJM AI, 2025), trained on over 10 million ECGs, and **CSFM** (Nature Machine Intelligence, 2026), which unifies hospital and smartwatch data, represent the state-of-the-art in diagnostic accuracy. However, external validation studies reveal a persistent "generalization gap." **DeepECG-SSL** (European Heart Journal, 2026) demonstrated that while self-supervised models outperform supervised baselines, they still exhibit performance drops across geographically distinct cohorts. Similarly, the **BenchECG** benchmark (arXiv, 2025) highlighted that cross-population evaluation remains limited to high-income countries, with the Global South (including South Asian and African cohorts) entirely absent from standard evaluations.

### 2.2 The Mechanism of Bias: Perception vs. Shortcut
The field currently operates under two competing hypotheses regarding *why* these models fail on diverse populations:
1.  **The Perceptual Deficit Hypothesis:** The model simply fails to recognize benign morphological variants common in specific populations (e.g., early repolarization patterns in young Black males) and misclassifies them as pathology.
2.  **The Prevalence-Proxy Shortcut Hypothesis:** The model correctly perceives the morphology but has learned a "shortcut" linking demographic features to disease prevalence priors.

Evidence from adjacent modalities supports the shortcut hypothesis. **Ghassemi et al.** (Nature Medicine, 2024) demonstrated that chest X-ray models use demographic "shortcuts" to predict disease, and crucially, that training-time debiasing methods fail to transfer between hospitals. In the ECG domain, **Bollepalli et al.** (npj Cardiovascular Health, 2025) analyzed 10 million ECGs and found that AI can predict race with 86% accuracy, but that these "race signatures" are largely **environmental** (driven by socioeconomic factors, nutrition, and chest wall thickness) rather than genetic. This finding is critical: if the demographic signal is environmental and variable, a model relying on it as a stable proxy for disease risk is fundamentally flawed.

### 2.3 The Interpretability Gap
Despite these findings, no study has opened the "black box" of an ECG foundation model to locate these shortcuts. Mechanistic interpretability tools, specifically **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)**, have recently been successfully applied to EEG foundation models (Lehn-Schiøler et al., arXiv 2605.13930, May 2026) to disentangle age-related features from pathology. However, this toolkit has **never been applied to ECG foundation models** for the purpose of cross-population fairness. Furthermore, a June 2026 study on ECG fairness (**DA-GAT-v2**, Scientific Reports) explicitly stated that cross-ethnic analysis on datasets with race labels (like MIMIC-IV) is "required" to substantiate fairness claims, confirming that this specific analysis remains a verified gap in the literature.

### 2.4 Inference-Time Interventions
Current mitigation strategies are almost exclusively **training-time** (e.g., adversarial suppression, reweighting, or fine-tuning), which require access to large, labeled target datasets—resources often unavailable in low-resource settings. In contrast, **inference-time** interventions like **LEACE** (Linear Erasure of Concept Embeddings; Belrose et al., NeurIPS 2023) allow for the surgical removal of specific concepts (e.g., "demographics") from a model’s internal representations using only unlabeled data. While proven in Large Language Models, LEACE has not yet been validated for medical time-series encoders.

## 3. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY

### Stage 1: Causal Decomposition (The "Autopsy")
We will mathematically partition the total cross-population performance gap ($\Delta$Metric) into five distinct causal components using a **Shapley-value attribution framework**:
1.  **Prevalence Shift:** Differences in disease base rates.
2.  **Acquisition Shift:** Differences in device/scanner/paper-vs-digital artifacts.
3.  **Benign Morphology:** Harmless variants misclassified as disease.
4.  **True Pathology:** Genuine biological differences in disease presentation.
5.  **Labeling Shift:** Differences in clinical annotation thresholds.

### Stage 2: Mechanistic Localization (The "Brain Scan")
We will train **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)** on the internal activations of open-weight models (ECG-FM, ECGFounder).
*   **Objective:** To identify specific "features" or "directions" in the latent space that encode demographic information.
*   **Causal Test:** We will perform **Activation Patching** (causal mediation analysis). We will take the "demographic activation" from a target-population patient and inject it into the processing stream of a source-population patient. If this injection alone causes the model to flip its diagnosis (e.g., from Healthy to Diseased), we will have causally proven the existence of the **Prevalence-Proxy Shortcut**.

### Stage 3: Surgical Erasure (The "Fix")

*   **Constraint:** We will enforce a **Physiological-Preservation Constraint**, verifying that the erasure does not degrade performance on unambiguous diagnostic cases (e.g., clear STEMI or Atrial Fibrillation).
*   **Comparison:** We will benchmark this label-free, inference-time fix against standard training-time baselines (fine-tuning, adversarial debiasing).

### Stage 4: Generalization
We will replicate the pipeline across three distinct foundation models (**ECG-FM, ECGFounder, CSFM**) and multiple ancestry axes to determine if the "Shortcut Hypothesis" is a universal law of physiological AI or a model-specific artifact.

% --- Abstract ---
\begin{abstract}
Electrocardiogram (ECG) foundation models, trained predominantly on data from a small number of high-income countries, exhibit degraded diagnostic performance when applied to patients from underrepresented populations. The prevailing explanation in the literature attributes this degradation to the misclassification of population-specific benign morphological variants as pathological findings. This explanation, however, has never been directly tested against an equally plausible alternative: that the model correctly perceives the underlying signal, but a learned association between population-identity features and disease prevalence introduces a systematic bias in its output, independent of the true morphology present. Distinguishing between these mechanisms matters because they imply fundamentally different remedies. This paper proposes a three-stage protocol to resolve the question directly. First, the aggregate cross-population performance gap is decomposed into five distinguishable causal components --- prevalence shift, acquisition shift, benign-morphology shift, true pathology-manifestation shift, and labeling shift --- using a Shapley-value attribution framework evaluated within single-site cohorts (MIMIC-IV-ECG and UK Biobank) so that acquisition conditions are held constant while ancestry varies. Second, sparse autoencoders are trained on the internal activations of open-weight ECG foundation models to identify candidate population-linked features, whose causal role is tested directly through activation patching rather than inferred from correlation alone. Third, where a causally implicated pathway is identified, a closed-form linear erasure technique is applied to remove it at inference time, without retraining and without labeled data from the target population, and the resulting model is evaluated for both fairness and diagnostic accuracy against existing correction methods. The full protocol, including its decision structure for every possible outcome, is pre-registered prior to analysis, so that a negative or contrary finding constitutes a publishable result in its own right rather than an unreportable failure. This work aims to provide the first mechanistic, causally validated account of cross-population failure in a physiological signal foundation model, together with a correction method that requires no population-specific retraining.
\end{abstract}

% --- 1. Introduction ---
\section{Introduction}
Artificial intelligence systems trained on electrocardiogram (ECG) signals have reached diagnostic performance comparable to subspecialist cardiologists on several tasks, and are increasingly proposed as a route to extending expert-level cardiac screening into settings without ready access to a cardiologist \cite{osullivan2026}. The overwhelming majority of the data underlying these systems, however, originates from a small number of high-income health systems in North America and Europe. When such models are evaluated on patients from outside this distribution, the emerging picture is consistent: performance degrades, and it degrades unevenly, in ways that correlate with demographic identity rather than with disease severity alone.

The response to this finding in the published literature has, to date, been largely descriptive. Studies document that a gap exists; comparatively little work asks what, mechanistically, produces it. This is a consequential omission, because the standard remedy proposed for demographic performance gaps --- collecting more diverse training data and retraining --- is expensive, slow, and in practice unavailable to the clinics most affected by the gap in the first place. If the gap has a specific, localizable cause inside the model rather than being an irreducible consequence of missing data, a more targeted and more rapidly deployable correction may be possible. This paper lays out a research protocol designed to determine whether that is the case for ECG foundation models specifically, and to build and test such a correction if it is.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item A five-way causal decomposition of the cross-population ECG performance gap, evaluated within single-site cohorts so that acquisition is held constant while ancestry varies.
    \item The first mechanistic, causally validated localization of population-linked shortcuts inside ECG foundation models, via sparse autoencoders and activation patching.
    \item An inference-time, label-free correction based on closed-form concept erasure, benchmarked against training-time debiasing on both fairness and diagnostic accuracy.
    \item A pre-registered decision structure under which every possible experimental outcome constitutes a publishable contribution.
\end{itemize}

% --- 2. Literature Review ---
\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Foundation Models for Electrocardiogram Interpretation}
A succession of large-scale ECG foundation models has been published in the past two years. ECGFounder (NEJM AI, 2025) is a supervised model trained on 10,771,552 ECGs from 1,818,247 patients drawn from the Harvard--Emory ECG database, achieving an AUROC above 0.95 across 80 of its 150 diagnostic labels. ECG-FM (JAMIA Open, 2025) takes a self-supervised approach, using contrastive multi-segment coding over 1.5 million ECGs, and is notable for releasing its weights openly. The Cardiac Sensing Foundation Model (Nature Machine Intelligence, 2026) extends the paradigm to roughly 1.7 million individuals by unifying clinical 12-lead ECG with consumer-device photoplethysmography, addressing cross-device rather than cross-population variation. DeepECG-SSL (European Heart Journal, 2026) directly compares self-supervised and supervised pretraining across eleven geographically distinct cohorts and finds the self-supervised variant more robust on low-data biomarker tasks, but the comparison is confined to measuring whether performance holds up across sites, not why it fails where it does.

A recurring property of this literature is that none of these models separates the encoding of diagnostic morphology from the encoding of population identity. Each is trained as a single, monolithic mapping from signal to diagnosis, and the demographic composition of the training population is absorbed into that mapping implicitly rather than represented as a distinct, inspectable quantity.

\subsection{Evidence of Cross-Population Performance Disparities}
Direct evidence that this absorption produces harm comes from several directions. In the clinical evaluation of large language models over cardiology cases, O'Sullivan et al.\ (2026) report a randomized trial in which an LLM-assisted diagnostic system reduced clinically significant errors from 24.3\% to 13.1\% among nine cardiologists, evaluated against a ten-domain blinded rubric; the authors note explicitly that their single-center, English-language sample provided insufficient demographic variation to test for bias, leaving the question open rather than resolved. In a South Asian cohort, the KERALA-AF study (Lancet Regional Health -- Southeast Asia, 2026) found that a model combining scanned paper ECGs with tabular clinical data achieved an AUC of 0.816 for one-year stroke risk, substantially above the standard CHA$_2$DS$_2$-VASc score's 0.666, with a permutation analysis attributing over half of the model's predictive signal to the scanned images specifically. Most directly, DA-GAT-v2 (Scientific Reports, 2026), a fairness-aware ECG architecture addressing sex and age bias, states in its own limitations section that its training data lacks race or ethnicity annotation, and that further evaluation on a dataset with explicit ethnicity labels --- naming MIMIC-IV-ECG specifically --- is required before any claim about cross-ethnic fairness can be substantiated. To date, no published study has undertaken that evaluation.

\subsection{Shortcut Learning as a Mechanism of Bias in Medical Imaging}
The clearest account of a mechanism, as opposed to a mere measurement, of demographic bias in medical AI comes from outside ECG entirely. Work on chest radiograph classifiers \cite{seyyed2021} established that these models exhibit systematically unequal false-negative rates across demographic subgroups. Subsequent work traced this to models learning demographic ``shortcuts'' --- features correlated with, but not causally relevant to, the diagnostic target \cite{shortcut2023} --- and reported a finding of particular importance to the present proposal: correcting these shortcuts at one hospital does not reliably transfer when the corrected model is deployed at a different hospital \cite{mitnews2024}. This is direct empirical evidence that a bias finding, and a bias fix, established in one clinical site or one modality cannot be assumed to generalize to another, which is the central reason the present study treats the ECG-specific question as open rather than settled by analogy to imaging.

\subsection{The Demographic Content of the ECG Signal}
Independent of the bias literature, there is direct evidence that population identity is linearly decodable from the ECG waveform itself. Bollepalli et al.\ \cite{bollepalli2025}, training on approximately ten million ECGs, report a classifier that predicts self-reported race at 86\% accuracy from the raw signal. Notably, classifier performance is weaker among lower-income patients and the decodable signal shifts with patient age, a pattern more consistent with an environmentally and socially mediated signal than a fixed, purely heritable one. This finding is significant for the present proposal because it means a population-identity signal is available, in principle, for a model to exploit as a shortcut; whether it is in fact exploited in this way, and whether doing so drives the diagnostic disparities described in Section 2.2, has not been tested.

\subsection{Existing Correction Approaches and Their Limitations}
Two directions of correction exist in the ECG literature, and both share the same structural limitation. DA-GAT-v2 addresses sex and age fairness through architectural modification and joint accuracy--fairness optimization during training. A separate line of work applies a variational autoencoder with an adversarial discriminator to strip demographic information from ECG embeddings, evaluated on a single downstream task and motivated primarily by re-identification privacy rather than diagnostic equity. Both approaches require retraining the model, both require labeled demographic data at training time, and, following the transfer-failure finding described in Section 2.3, there is no existing evidence that either would generalize to a population not represented during that retraining. No inference-time correction, applicable to an already-deployed, frozen model without new labeled data, has been proposed for any ECG model.

\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability and Causal Intervention}
A separate body of work, originating largely in natural language processing, offers tools that address exactly this limitation, though none has yet been applied to a cardiac signal. Sparse autoencoders decompose a neural network's internal activations, which typically conflate many unrelated concepts within individual neurons (a property termed superposition), into a larger set of directions each corresponding to a more cleanly interpretable concept \cite{marks2025}. Critically, this line of work pairs feature discovery with causal intervention --- directly manipulating a candidate feature and observing whether model output changes --- rather than relying on the correlational attribution methods (saliency maps, SHAP) that have been shown to sometimes produce plausible-looking explanations that do not reflect the model's actual computation \cite{adebayo2018}. Once a causal feature is confirmed, LEACE \cite{belrose2023} provides a closed-form method for removing that feature's linear signature from a model's representations without retraining. The first demonstration that this toolkit functions on a physiological, rather than purely linguistic, signal is recent: Lehn-Schi\o ler et al.\ \cite{lehn2026} applied sparse autoencoders to electroencephalogram (EEG) foundation models and identified a genuine age--pathology confound inside the model's internal representations. This result establishes feasibility for physiological time-series models in general; it has not been extended to ECG, nor to the specific question of population-linked shortcuts.

A related but distinct thread has begun to formalize the decomposition of performance drops into their constituent causes. Roschewitz et al.\ \cite{roschewitz2024} provide a statistical test distinguishing prevalence shift from covariate shift in medical imaging, though their framework does not separate acquisition-related covariate shift from population-related covariate shift, treating both as a single category. Gordaliza et al.\ \cite{gordaliza2025} go further within imaging specifically, using a Shapley-value attribution to split a segmentation model's performance drop between scanner-acquisition and annotator-labeling causes. Their decomposition, however, is limited to two mechanisms and does not include a term for genuine population-level physiological difference or benign morphological variation, and has not been applied outside imaging.

% --- 3. Synthesis and Research Gap ---
\section{Synthesis and Research Gap}
Taken together, the literature reviewed above establishes that a cross-population performance gap in ECG foundation models is real (Section 2.2), that a mechanistically similar gap in a different modality is driven by learned demographic shortcuts that do not reliably transfer across sites (Section 2.3), that population identity is decodable from the ECG signal in a manner consistent with a usable shortcut (Section 2.4), that every existing correction is training-time and site-specific (Section 2.5), and that a toolkit capable of causally locating and surgically removing exactly this kind of shortcut exists and has been shown to work on a physiological, though not yet cardiac, signal (Section 2.6). No published or preprint work known to the author combines these elements. Specifically, five distinct gaps follow directly from the review above:

\noindent \textbf{First,} no study has decomposed the ECG cross-population gap into more than two causal components, and no study has done so for population-level causes specifically, as opposed to acquisition and annotation alone (Section 2.6).

\noindent \textbf{Second,} no study has identified the internal mechanism responsible for cross-population ECG failure; all existing evidence is behavioral rather than causal (Sections 2.2, 2.6).

\noindent \textbf{Third,} no inference-time, label-free correction exists for any ECG model, and the literature's own transfer-failure finding indicates the training-time alternatives now in use are unlikely to generalize (Sections 2.3, 2.5).

\noindent \textbf{Fourth,} the field's operating assumption --- that cross-population failure reflects misperception of benign morphology --- has never been tested against the alternative that the model perceives correctly but applies a learned, population-linked prior incorrectly (Sections 2.2, 2.4).

\noindent \textbf{Fifth,} no foundation-model benchmark includes a South Asian cohort at meaningful scale, a resource gap rather than a methodological one (Section 2.2).

The protocol described in Section 4 is designed to close the first four gaps directly and to route around the fifth without requiring it to be solved first.

% --- 4. Proposed Methodology ---
\section{Proposed Methodology}

\subsection{Data}
The design deliberately compares ancestry groups within a single data-collection protocol rather than across countries, so that any measured gap cannot be attributed to differing equipment or clinical workflow. MIMIC-IV-ECG \cite{johnson2023} provides 795,546 ECGs from 159,608 patients at a single United States hospital system, with race and ethnicity recorded for every patient (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, and finer subcategories). UK Biobank \cite{bycroft2018} separately provides a resting 12-lead ECG substudy of approximately 90,000 participants, within which genetic ancestry analysis has identified a group of 2,782 individuals of predominantly South Asian ancestry, recruited and scanned under the same protocol as the remainder of the cohort. The exact overlap between the ECG substudy and the South Asian ancestry group has not yet been confirmed and will be established before the analysis is finalized, as it determines the statistical power available for that comparison. Both datasets are public or credentialed-access resources; no new clinical data-sharing agreement is required for the core analysis.

\subsection{Phase 1: Causal Decomposition of the Performance Gap}
Within each dataset, model performance is compared across ancestry groups while site and acquisition device are held fixed. The observed gap is attributed across five candidate causes using a Shapley-value framework, in which each cause's contribution is its average marginal effect across all $2^5 = 32$ subsets of the five candidate causes, guaranteeing that the five attributed values sum to exactly the total measured gap. Prevalence shift is estimated by reweighting the target group's labels to the source group's disease base rate and remeasuring; acquisition shift is estimated by rendering digital signals as realistic scanned-paper images using the ECG-Image-Kit toolkit (Physiological Measurement, 2024) and remeasuring; benign-morphology and true-pathology shift are estimated respectively from the ancestry gap in false-positive rate among confirmed-healthy patients and true-positive rate among confirmed-disease patients; labeling shift is estimated by harmonizing diagnostic thresholds across sites and remeasuring.

\subsection{Phase 2: Mechanistic Localization}
Sparse autoencoders are trained on the internal activations of open-weight ECG foundation models (ECG-FM, ECGFounder), following the protocol demonstrated on EEG foundation models by Lehn-Schi\o ler et al.\ \cite{lehn2026}. Linear probes are trained on the resulting feature space to identify candidate directions predictive of ancestry. Each candidate is then tested causally through activation patching: the candidate feature extracted from one patient's ECG is substituted into the model's representation of a different, otherwise-unmodified patient's ECG, and the resulting change, or absence of change, in diagnostic output is recorded. A diagnosis that flips as a direct result of the substitution constitutes causal evidence that the feature drives the model's output, as distinct from merely correlating with it, addressing the reliability limitations documented for correlational attribution methods (Section 2.6).

\subsection{Phase 3: Inference-Time Correction}
Where Phase 2 identifies a causally implicated feature, LEACE \cite{belrose2023} is applied to remove that feature's linear signature from the model's activations at the moment of inference, computed in closed form and requiring no modification to the model's trained weights and no additional labeled data. The corrected model is evaluated against a physiological-preservation criterion --- confirming that unambiguous pathology (for example, ST-elevation myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation) remains correctly detected after correction --- and is compared head-to-head against the existing training-time approaches described in Section 2.5 on both fairness and raw diagnostic accuracy.

\subsection{Phase 4: Generalization}
To establish that any finding from Phases 1 through 3 reflects a general property rather than an artifact of one dataset or one model, the full protocol is repeated on the second ancestry axis not used as the primary analysis (MIMIC-IV or UK Biobank, whichever was not primary) and on a third open-weight foundation model, the Cardiac Sensing Foundation Model. A finding that replicates across both ancestry comparisons and across model architectures is reported as evidence of a general mechanism; a finding that does not replicate is reported as a boundary condition, and is itself an informative result about the scope of the phenomenon.

\subsection{Pre-registration and Outcome Independence}
The hypothesis, the five-way decomposition definitions, and the decision structure mapping each possible experimental outcome to its corresponding conclusion are time-stamped and registered prior to any data analysis (OSF / AsPredicted). The pre-specified mapping is as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item If a causally sufficient population-linked shortcut is identified and erasure closes the equity gap without accuracy loss, the result is a label-free, retraining-free correction method.
    \item If erasure closes the gap but at a measurable accuracy cost, the result is a characterization of the fundamental accuracy--equity trade-off and its mechanistic origin.
    \item If no causally sufficient shortcut exists, the result is evidence that ECG cross-population failure is perceptual rather than shortcut-driven --- a finding that contradicts the imaging literature and redirects the field accordingly.
    \item If the gap is dominated by acquisition or labeling artifacts, the result is a harmonization-based remedy and a re-framing of the field's benign-variant assumption.
\end{itemize}
This structure is intended to ensure that a result contrary to the study's central hypothesis is reported as a substantive contribution to the field's understanding rather than treated as an unpublishable negative result.

\subsection{Anticipated Limitations}
Several limitations are acknowledged up front. The UK Biobank overlap between the ECG substudy and the South Asian ancestry group must be confirmed before that comparison is powered, and underpowered cells will be treated as exploratory. Sparse autoencoder features can be entangled or seed-sensitive; the protocol therefore reports reproducible subspaces across multiple seeds and treats entanglement itself as a finding rather than a failure. LEACE removes only linearly accessible information; should probing reveal non-linear encoding, the protocol escalates to non-linear erasure or targeted feature ablation, as pre-specified. Finally, as a secondary analysis of public data, the work establishes mechanistic and methodological results rather than prospective clinical validation, which is named as the required next step rather than implied.

% --- 5. Significance ---
\section{Significance}
If the central hypothesis is confirmed, this work would provide the first mechanistic account of why cross-population failure occurs in a cardiac foundation model, and a correction deployable without retraining or new labeled data, of particular relevance to clinical settings where neither is readily available. If the hypothesis is not confirmed, the work would still establish, for the first time with causal rather than correlational evidence, that ECG cross-population failure does not follow the demographic-shortcut pattern documented for medical imaging, redirecting subsequent work toward genuine representation differences rather than bias-correction techniques that would not apply. Independent of either outcome, the five-way causal decomposition, the mechanistic interpretability toolkit adapted for a 1-D physiological signal, and the cross-ancestry benchmark constructed across MIMIC-IV and UK Biobank constitute standalone contributions to the field.

\begin{abstract}
Electrocardiogram (ECG) models trained using artificial intelligence (AI) can now approach cardiologist-level interpretation, but most are trained on data collected in North America and Europe. These models perform worse on patients from other populations, and this drop correlates more strongly with the model's difficulty in distinguishing normal cardiac characteristics from disease-related patterns. The model might read the heart signal correctly, but it may still link a patient's background to the odds of disease. The usual fix, collecting more diverse data and retraining the model, takes a lot of time and is expensive. If the cause turns out to be a locatable signal, a cheaper fix becomes possible. This paper proposes a four-phase pipeline: decomposing the gap into its causes, identifying the responsible signal, correcting it at the point of use, and verifying that the fix generalises. The first phase splits the performance gap into five parts: disease rate, scan type, harmless traits, real disease signs, and labelling rules, using Shapley-value decomposition, so the five shares always add up to the full gap. The second phase opens the model to find the exact signal at fault, using a sparse autoencoder, which pulls out clear, single-purpose signals from the model's inner workings. Each candidate signal is then swapped between patients to test whether it drives the output. The third step removes any confirmed signal at the moment of use with LEACE (LEAst-squares Concept Erasure), a closed-form correction that needs no retraining and no new patient labels. The corrected model is then checked for both fairness and accuracy. The fourth phase repeats this whole chain on a held-out population and two additional ECG models to confirm that the finding holds up in general.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} rapid growth of artificial intelligence (AI) has led to ECG interpretation tools that can perform almost as well as cardiologists. These tools have the potential to improve healthcare in areas where heart specialists are not available. However, most AI models are trained on data collected from developed countries, mainly in North America and Europe. When tested on other populations, their performance often decreases. Research shows that this decrease is more strongly related to demographic data of the patient than to the severity of the disease.

Most published work simply notes that this gap exists, while only a few of them ask what inside the model actually causes it. That gap in understanding matters. The usual answer, collect more diverse data and retrain, costs money and time neither always available. If the gap traces to one specific, locatable cause, a faster and cheaper fix becomes possible. This report lays out a plan built to test that idea directly, specifically for ECG models, and to build a fix if the idea holds. The rest of this section reviews related ECG models and bias research, and then explains in detail how this paper's contributions close the gaps found in that research.

\subsection{Foundation Models Built for ECG Reading}
Several large ECG models have been released in the past two years. ECGFounder, published in NEJM AI in 2025, learned from over 10 million ECG recordings drawn from the Harvard-Emory database, and reaches strong accuracy across 80 separate heart conditions. ECG-FM, published in JAMIA Open in 2025, learns by comparing signal segments against each other, and its code is public. The Cardiac Sensing Foundation Model, published in Nature Machine Intelligence in 2026, trained on data from about 1.7 million people, and mixes hospital-grade ECG with smartwatch sensor data. DeepECG-SSL, published in the European Heart Journal in 2026, compared label-free and labeled training across eleven separate patient groups worldwide. The label-free version held up better on small, hard tasks. That study checked whether the models still worked. It never asked why they sometimes failed.

Every one of these models shares a gap. None separates true heart signal from patient background inside its own workings. Group traits from the training data get folded silently into how the model reads a signal.

\subsection{Evidence That the Gap Causes Real Harm}
Direct evidence backs this concern. O'Sullivan and colleagues tested a large language model on real cardiology cases in 2026. Doctors assisted by the model made fewer errors. The trial used one hospital and English-speaking patients only. The authors could not test for bias across backgrounds, by their own account. The KERALA-AF study \cite{keralaaf2026}, published in 2026, looked at South Asian patients with an irregular heartbeat condition. A model combining scanned paper ECGs with clinical data beat the standard risk score by a wide margin. The scanned images alone supplied most of the useful signal. DA-GAT-v2, a 2026 model built for fairness by age and sex, states plainly that its own training data lacks race labels. Its authors call for testing on data that has such labels, and name MIMIC-IV-ECG directly as the right dataset for that test. No published study has run that test yet.

\subsection{Shortcut Learning in Medical Image Models}
The clearest account of how such bias actually works comes from chest X-ray research, not ECG. A widely cited 2021 study found X-ray models miss disease more often in certain patient groups. Follow-up work traced this to the model learning group-linked shortcuts, patterns tied to a patient's background rather than to disease itself. A 2024 finding, reported through Nature Medicine and MIT News, matters most here. Fixing these shortcuts at one hospital did not carry over to a different hospital. A bias fix proven in one place can fail somewhere else. This is the direct reason ECG needs its own, separate study, rather than borrowing conclusions from image models.

\subsection{Patient Background Encoded in the Raw ECG Signal}
Separate work shows patient background sits directly inside the ECG waveform. Bollepalli and colleagues, training on close to ten million ECGs, built a model that guesses a patient's race correctly 86 percent of the time from the signal alone. The model does worse on lower-income patients, and its accuracy shifts as patients age. Both patterns point toward a signal shaped by environment and society, not fixed biology alone. This matters here because it means a background-linked signal is available for a diagnostic model to lean on as a shortcut. Whether ECG models actually do this has never been directly tested.

\subsection{Tools for Locating and Editing Causes Inside a Model}
A separate toolkit, built mostly for language models, offers a way past this limit. It has not yet reached ECG. Sparse autoencoders untangle a model's internal activity, which normally blends many unrelated signals inside single neurons, into a larger set of cleaner, single-purpose signals. These tools pair discovery with direct testing: a candidate signal gets changed on purpose, and the output is checked for a real change. This beats older methods that only guess at relevance from correlation, which past work has shown can mislead. A method called LEACE removes a confirmed bad signal from a model's internal representation, using closed-form math, without retraining. Lehn-Schi{\o}ler and colleagues recently ran sparse autoencoders on brain-wave (EEG) models, and found a genuine mix-up between patient age and disease state inside the model. That result proves the toolkit works on body signals generally. It has not been used on ECG, or on background-linked bias specifically.

A related thread has tried to split model performance drops into separate causes. Roschewitz and colleagues built a 2024 statistical test that separates disease-rate shifts from other shifts in image models, though it groups scanner changes and patient-background changes into one bucket. Gordaliza and colleagues, in 2025, used a fair-scoring math method to split an image model's performance drop between scanner changes and labeling changes, two causes only, leaving out true biological difference and harmless trait variation entirely. Neither method has been applied to ECG.

\subsection{Contributions of This Work}
The studies described above point to five clear gaps in current ECG bias research. This paper is built to close each of these gaps directly. The plan used in this paper has four phases, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} in the Methodology section: splitting the gap into causes, finding the exact signal responsible, correcting that signal at the point of use, and checking that the correction still works on new data and new models. Each contribution below lines up with one part of this plan, and the fifth contribution adds a new test cohort. In total, this paper makes five main contributions.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=6pt]
    \item \textbf{Splitting the ECG performance gap into five separate, measurable causes.} Work that tries to split a model's performance drop into separate causes exists only for medical images, not for ECG, and even there it only splits the drop into two causes at a time. Roschewitz and colleagues built a statistical test that separates disease-rate shifts from other shifts in image models, but it groups scanner changes and patient-background changes into one bucket \cite{roschewitz2024}. Gordaliza and colleagues split an image model's drop between scanner changes and labeling changes only, leaving out true biological difference and harmless trait variation completely \cite{gordaliza2025}. Neither method has been applied to ECG. Phase 1 of this paper closes this gap using Shapley-value decomposition, a fair-scoring method from game theory that gives each of five candidate causes, disease rate, scan type, harmless traits, true disease severity, and labelling rules, its own share of the total gap. Because Shapley values always add up correctly, the five shares sum exactly to the full measured gap, with nothing left unexplained.

    \item \textbf{Locating the exact internal signal behind group-linked bias, and testing it directly instead of guessing.} It is already known that a patient's background can be read from a raw ECG signal with high accuracy \cite{bollepalli2025}. But that alone does not prove a diagnostic model actually uses this information when it makes a decision. The tool needed to test this directly, sparse autoencoders combined with causal editing, has so far only been used on brain-wave (EEG) models, and never on ECG \cite{lehn2026}. Phase 2 of this paper trains this tool on ECG models, then swaps a candidate signal between two patients' ECG recordings and checks whether the model's diagnosis changes as a result. A change in diagnosis is direct proof that the signal drives the output, rather than just sitting alongside it by coincidence.

    \item \textbf{Building a fix that works at the point of use, needing no retraining and no new patient data.} No existing fix has been shown to work outside the hospital it was built for. Fixes such as DA-GAT-v2 and GAN-based signal stripping both need full retraining and need patient background labels ahead of time. A related finding in chest X-ray models showed that a bias fix proven at one hospital did not carry over to a different hospital \cite{mitnews2024}. This same generalization problem has never been addressed for ECG. Phase 3 of this paper works differently. Once Phase 2 confirms which internal signal drives the bias, LEACE (LEAst-squares Concept Erasure) removes exactly that signal at the moment a prediction is made, not during training. In plain terms, the model is calibrated once on a small reference set of about a thousand ECGs. This step learns one internal number that represents how strongly a signal looks tied to one population. From then on, that number is subtracted out of every new patient's ECG right before the model gives its final answer. The model's trained weights never change, and no new patient labels are needed once the model is in use.

    \item \textbf{Testing a second explanation for ECG bias that nobody has checked before.} Existing studies all assume that a model's accuracy drops on a new population because the model misreads the ECG signal itself \cite{seyyed2021,osullivan2026}. An equally likely second explanation has never been tested. The model might read the signal correctly, but then apply a learned assumption about how common a disease is in a certain population, and that assumption may simply be wrong for the patient in front of it. The causal-patching step inside Phase 2 is built to tell these two explanations apart for the first time, by checking whether swapping a background-linked signal changes the diagnosis on its own.

    \item \textbf{Building a fair test set for South Asian patients, a group almost missing from ECG AI research today.} The standardized benchmark BenchECG draws its cohorts only from Europe, the USA, China, and Brazil \cite{benchecg2025}. A 2026 cardiovascular foundation model states outright that its performance on African and Latin American patients has not been tested \cite{ecgclip2026}. The closest thing to a South Asian ECG resource, KERALA-AF, is a risk-score validation study that uses scanned paper images, not a foundation-model benchmark \cite{keralaaf2026}. Phase 4 of this paper closes this gap by building a South Asian test cohort from the UK Biobank's genetically identified subgroup, about 2,782 people, and checking whether the Phase 3 fix, and the underlying finding, hold up across three separate ECG foundation models.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} lines up the two existing decomposition methods and the two existing fixes described above against four of these gaps (G1 to G4). None of the existing methods meet all four at once. The fifth gap, on Global South coverage, is a question of data availability rather than method design, and is addressed directly by the Phase 4 cohort described above.

\subsection{Data}
The design compares patient background groups inside a single hospital or study, not across different countries. This keeps scan type and clinical routine fixed, so a measured gap can only trace to the patients themselves. MIMIC-IV-ECG supplies 795{,}546 ECG recordings from 159{,}608 patients at one United States hospital system, with race and ethnicity recorded for every patient. UK Biobank separately holds a resting ECG record for roughly 90{,}000 participants, and its genetic data identifies 2{,}782 people of mostly South Asian background within that group, all scanned under the same study protocol as everyone else. The exact overlap between the ECG records and that South Asian group is not yet confirmed, and will be checked before final analysis, since it decides whether that comparison has enough patients behind it. Both datasets are open to researchers already. No new data-sharing agreement is needed for the core analysis.

\subsection{Phase 1: Splitting the Gap into Causes}
Model accuracy is compared across patient background groups within each dataset, holding site and scan device fixed. A Shapley-value method credits each of five candidate causes with its fair share of the measured gap, based on the model's behavior across every possible combination of the five causes (32 combinations for five causes), so the five shares always add up to the full gap. Disease-rate differences are estimated by matching one group's disease rate to the other's and re-scoring. Scan-type differences are estimated by turning digital signals into realistic scanned-paper images, using the ECG-Image-Kit toolkit, and re-scoring. Harmless-trait differences are read from the gap in false alarms among confirmed-healthy patients. True disease-difference is read from the gap in correct detections among confirmed-sick patients. Labeling-rule differences are estimated by matching every site's disease threshold to one shared standard and re-scoring.

\subsection{Phase 2: Locating the Exact Cause}
Sparse autoencoders are trained on the internal activity of open, downloadable ECG models, following the same steps already shown to work on brain-wave models. Simple linear probes then search that activity for a signal tied to patient background. Each candidate signal is tested directly, not just observed: a signal drawn from one patient's ECG is inserted into a different patient's ECG scan inside the model, with every other detail held constant, and the model's output is checked for a change. A shift in diagnosis proves that signal actually drives the output, not merely that it sits alongside it.

\subsection{Phase 3: Fixing the Model at the Point of Use}
Where Phase 2 confirms a real cause, LEACE removes that exact signal from the model's internal activity at the moment of use. The model's trained weights never change, and no new labeled data is required. The fixed model is then checked to confirm it still catches clear, unmistakable disease, such as a major heart attack. Its fairness and its raw accuracy are then compared directly against the retraining-based fixes already in use.

\subsection{Phase 4: Testing Beyond One Dataset}
To show the finding holds generally, and is not a one-off, the entire chain above is repeated on the second patient-background comparison not used first, and on a third open ECG model, the Cardiac Sensing Foundation Model. A result that holds across both comparisons and both extra models counts as a general pattern. A result that does not hold marks a real boundary, and is informative either way.

\section{Significance}
If the main idea holds, this work gives the field its first real, tested cause for ECG bias across patient groups, plus a fix that skips retraining and new data collection entirely, useful anywhere resources are limited. If the main idea does not hold, that still teaches the field something concrete: that ECG bias does not work the same way image-model bias does, which redirects future effort toward genuine biological difference instead of a shortcut fix that would not apply. Either way, the five-cause split, the interpretability toolkit adapted for a body signal, and the new cross-background test set built from MIMIC-IV and UK Biobank stand as real, usable contributions on their own.

\begin{abstract}
Electrocardiogram (ECG) models trained using artificial intelligence (AI) can now approach cardiologist-level interpretation, but most are trained on data collected in North America and Europe. These models perform worse on patients from other populations, and this drop correlates more strongly with the model's difficulty in distinguishing normal cardiac characteristics from disease-related patterns. The model might read the heart signal correctly, but it may still link a patient's background to the odds of disease. The usual fix, collecting more diverse data and retraining the model, takes a lot of time and is expensive. If the cause turns out to be a locatable signal, a cheaper fix becomes possible. This paper proposes a four-phase pipeline: decomposing the gap into its causes, identifying the responsible signal, correcting it at the point of use, and verifying that the fix generalises. The first phase splits the performance gap into five parts: disease rate, scan type, harmless traits, real disease signs, and labelling rules, using Shapley-value decomposition, so the five shares always add up to the full gap. The second phase opens the model to find the exact signal at fault, using a sparse autoencoder, which pulls out clear, single-purpose signals from the model's inner workings. Each candidate signal is then swapped between patients to test whether it drives the output. The third step removes any confirmed signal at the moment of use with LEACE (LEAst-squares Concept Erasure), a closed-form correction that needs no retraining and no new patient labels. The corrected model is then checked for both fairness and accuracy. The fourth phase repeats this whole chain on a held-out population and two additional ECG models to confirm that the finding holds up in general.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} rapid growth of artificial intelligence (AI) has led to ECG interpretation tools that can perform almost as well as cardiologists. These tools have the potential to improve healthcare in areas where heart specialists are not available. However, most AI models are trained on data collected from developed countries, mainly in North America and Europe. When tested on other populations, their performance often decreases. Research shows that this decrease is more strongly related to demographic data of the patient than to the severity of the disease.

Most published work simply notes that this gap exists, while only a few of them ask what inside the model actually causes it. That gap in understanding matters. The usual answer, collect more diverse data and retrain, costs money and time neither always available. If the gap traces to one specific, locatable cause, a faster and cheaper fix becomes possible. This report lays out a plan built to test that idea directly, specifically for ECG models, and to build a fix if the idea holds. The rest of this section reviews related ECG models and bias research, and then explains in detail how this paper's contributions close the gaps found in that research.

\subsection{Foundation Models Built for ECG Reading}
Several large ECG models have been released in the past two years. ECGFounder, published in NEJM AI in 2025, learned from over 10 million ECG recordings drawn from the Harvard-Emory database, and reaches strong accuracy across 80 separate heart conditions. ECG-FM, published in JAMIA Open in 2025, learns by comparing signal segments against each other, and its code is public. The Cardiac Sensing Foundation Model, published in Nature Machine Intelligence in 2026, trained on data from about 1.7 million people, and mixes hospital-grade ECG with smartwatch sensor data. DeepECG-SSL, published in the European Heart Journal in 2026, compared label-free and labeled training across eleven separate patient groups worldwide. The label-free version held up better on small, hard tasks. That study checked whether the models still worked. It never asked why they sometimes failed.

Every one of these models shares a gap. None separates true heart signal from patient background inside its own workings. Group traits from the training data get folded silently into how the model reads a signal.

\subsection{Evidence That the Gap Causes Real Harm}
Direct evidence backs this concern. O'Sullivan and colleagues tested a large language model on real cardiology cases in 2026. Doctors assisted by the model made fewer errors. The trial used one hospital and English-speaking patients only. The authors could not test for bias across backgrounds, by their own account. The KERALA-AF study \cite{keralaaf2026}, published in 2026, looked at South Asian patients with an irregular heartbeat condition. A model combining scanned paper ECGs with clinical data beat the standard risk score by a wide margin. The scanned images alone supplied most of the useful signal. DA-GAT-v2, a 2026 model built for fairness by age and sex, states plainly that its own training data lacks race labels. Its authors call for testing on data that has such labels, and name MIMIC-IV-ECG directly as the right dataset for that test. No published study has run that test yet.

\subsection{Shortcut Learning in Medical Image Models}
The clearest account of how such bias actually works comes from chest X-ray research, not ECG. A widely cited 2021 study found X-ray models miss disease more often in certain patient groups. Follow-up work traced this to the model learning group-linked shortcuts, patterns tied to a patient's background rather than to disease itself. A 2024 finding, reported through Nature Medicine and MIT News, matters most here. Fixing these shortcuts at one hospital did not carry over to a different hospital. A bias fix proven in one place can fail somewhere else. This is the direct reason ECG needs its own, separate study, rather than borrowing conclusions from image models.

\subsection{Patient Background Encoded in the Raw ECG Signal}
Separate work shows patient background sits directly inside the ECG waveform. Bollepalli and colleagues, training on close to ten million ECGs, built a model that guesses a patient's race correctly 86 percent of the time from the signal alone. The model does worse on lower-income patients, and its accuracy shifts as patients age. Both patterns point toward a signal shaped by environment and society, not fixed biology alone. This matters here because it means a background-linked signal is available for a diagnostic model to lean on as a shortcut. Whether ECG models actually do this has never been directly tested.

\subsection{Tools for Locating and Editing Causes Inside a Model}
A separate toolkit, built mostly for language models, offers a way past this limit. It has not yet reached ECG. Sparse autoencoders untangle a model's internal activity, which normally blends many unrelated signals inside single neurons, into a larger set of cleaner, single-purpose signals. These tools pair discovery with direct testing: a candidate signal gets changed on purpose, and the output is checked for a real change. This beats older methods that only guess at relevance from correlation, which past work has shown can mislead. A method called LEACE removes a confirmed bad signal from a model's internal representation, using closed-form math, without retraining. Lehn-Schi{\o}ler and colleagues recently ran sparse autoencoders on brain-wave (EEG) models, and found a genuine mix-up between patient age and disease state inside the model. That result proves the toolkit works on body signals generally. It has not been used on ECG, or on background-linked bias specifically.

A related thread has tried to split model performance drops into separate causes. Roschewitz and colleagues built a 2024 statistical test that separates disease-rate shifts from other shifts in image models, though it groups scanner changes and patient-background changes into one bucket. Gordaliza and colleagues, in 2025, used a fair-scoring math method to split an image model's performance drop between scanner changes and labeling changes, two causes only, leaving out true biological difference and harmless trait variation entirely. Neither method has been applied to ECG.

\subsection{Contributions of This Work}
The studies described above point to five clear gaps in current ECG bias research. This paper is built to close each of these gaps directly. The plan used in this paper has four phases, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} in the Methodology section: splitting the gap into causes, finding the exact signal responsible, correcting that signal at the point of use, and checking that the correction still works on new data and new models. Each contribution below lines up with one part of this plan, and the fifth contribution adds a new test cohort. In total, this paper makes five main contributions.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=6pt]
    \item \textbf{Splitting the ECG performance gap into five separate, measurable causes.} Work that tries to split a model's performance drop into separate causes exists only for medical images, not for ECG, and even there it only splits the drop into two causes at a time. Roschewitz and colleagues built a statistical test that separates disease-rate shifts from other shifts in image models, but it groups scanner changes and patient-background changes into one bucket \cite{roschewitz2024}. Gordaliza and colleagues split an image model's drop between scanner changes and labeling changes only, leaving out true biological difference and harmless trait variation completely \cite{gordaliza2025}. Neither method has been applied to ECG. Phase 1 of this paper closes this gap using Shapley-value decomposition, a fair-scoring method from game theory that gives each of five candidate causes, disease rate, scan type, harmless traits, true disease severity, and labelling rules, its own share of the total gap. Because Shapley values always add up correctly, the five shares sum exactly to the full measured gap, with nothing left unexplained.

    \item \textbf{Locating the exact internal signal behind group-linked bias, and testing it directly instead of guessing.} It is already known that a patient's background can be read from a raw ECG signal with high accuracy \cite{bollepalli2025}. But that alone does not prove a diagnostic model actually uses this information when it makes a decision. The tool needed to test this directly, sparse autoencoders combined with causal editing, has so far only been used on brain-wave (EEG) models, and never on ECG \cite{lehn2026}. Phase 2 of this paper trains this tool on ECG models, then swaps a candidate signal between two patients' ECG recordings and checks whether the model's diagnosis changes as a result. A change in diagnosis is direct proof that the signal drives the output, rather than just sitting alongside it by coincidence.

A related finding in chest X-ray models showed that a bias fix proven at one hospital did not carry over to a different hospital \cite{mitnews2024}. This same generalization problem has never been addressed for ECG. Phase 3 of this paper works differently. Once Phase 2 confirms which internal signal drives the bias, LEACE (LEAst-squares Concept Erasure) removes exactly that signal at the moment a prediction is made, not during training. In plain terms, the model is calibrated once on a small reference set of about a thousand ECGs. This step learns one internal number that represents how strongly a signal looks tied to one population. From then on, that number is subtracted out of every new patient's ECG right before the model gives its final answer. The model's trained weights never change, and no new patient labels are needed once the model is in use.

    \item \textbf{Testing a second explanation for ECG bias that nobody has checked before.} Existing studies all assume that a model's accuracy drops on a new population because the model misreads the ECG signal itself \cite{seyyed2021,osullivan2026}. An equally likely second explanation has never been tested. The model might read the signal correctly, but then apply a learned assumption about how common a disease is in a certain population, and that assumption may simply be wrong for the patient in front of it. The causal-patching step inside Phase 2 is built to tell these two explanations apart for the first time, by checking whether swapping a background-linked signal changes the diagnosis on its own.

\subsection{Data}
The design compares patient background groups inside a single hospital or study, not across different countries. This keeps scan type and clinical routine fixed, so a measured gap can only trace to the patients themselves. MIMIC-IV-ECG supplies 795{,}546 ECG recordings from 159{,}608 patients at one United States hospital system, with race and ethnicity recorded for every patient. UK Biobank separately holds a resting ECG record for roughly 90{,}000 participants, and its genetic data identifies 2{,}782 people of mostly South Asian background within that group, all scanned under the same study protocol as everyone else. The exact overlap between the ECG records and that South Asian group is not yet confirmed, and will be checked before final analysis, since it decides whether that comparison has enough patients behind it. Both datasets are open to researchers already. No new data-sharing agreement is needed for the core analysis.

\subsection{Phase 1: Splitting the Gap into Causes}
Model accuracy is compared across patient background groups within each dataset, holding site and scan device fixed. A Shapley-value method credits each of five candidate causes with its fair share of the measured gap, based on the model's behavior across every possible combination of the five causes (32 combinations for five causes), so the five shares always add up to the full gap. Disease-rate differences are estimated by matching one group's disease rate to the other's and re-scoring. Scan-type differences are estimated by turning digital signals into realistic scanned-paper images, using the ECG-Image-Kit toolkit, and re-scoring. Harmless-trait differences are read from the gap in false alarms among confirmed-healthy patients. True disease-difference is read from the gap in correct detections among confirmed-sick patients. Labeling-rule differences are estimated by matching every site's disease threshold to one shared standard and re-scoring.

\subsection{Phase 2: Locating the Exact Cause}
Sparse autoencoders are trained on the internal activity of open, downloadable ECG models, following the same steps already shown to work on brain-wave models. Simple linear probes then search that activity for a signal tied to patient background. Each candidate signal is tested directly, not just observed: a signal drawn from one patient's ECG is inserted into a different patient's ECG scan inside the model, with every other detail held constant, and the model's output is checked for a change. A shift in diagnosis proves that signal actually drives the output, not merely that it sits alongside it.

\subsection{Phase 3: Fixing the Model at the Point of Use}
Where Phase 2 confirms a real cause, LEACE removes that exact signal from the model's internal activity at the moment of use. The model's trained weights never change, and no new labeled data is required. The fixed model is then checked to confirm it still catches clear, unmistakable disease, such as a major heart attack. Its fairness and its raw accuracy are then compared directly against the retraining-based fixes already in use.

\subsection{Phase 4: Testing Beyond One Dataset}
To show the finding holds generally, and is not a one-off, the entire chain above is repeated on the second patient-background comparison not used first, and on a third open ECG model, the Cardiac Sensing Foundation Model. A result that holds across both comparisons and both extra models counts as a general pattern. A result that does not hold marks a real boundary, and is informative either way.

\section{Significance}
If the main idea holds, this work gives the field its first real, tested cause for ECG bias across patient groups, plus a fix that skips retraining and new data collection entirely, useful anywhere resources are limited. If the main idea does not hold, that still teaches the field something concrete: that ECG bias does not work the same way image-model bias does, which redirects future effort toward genuine biological difference instead of a shortcut fix that would not apply. Either way, the five-cause split, the interpretability toolkit adapted for a body signal, and the new cross-background test set built from MIMIC-IV and UK Biobank stand as real, usable contributions on their own.
