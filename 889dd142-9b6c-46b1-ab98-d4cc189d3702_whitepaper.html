Cross-Population Generalization in Cardiac AI

What's already known, what's genuinely missing, and what we're proposing to do about it
Prepared by: [Your name]  •  Prepared for: discussion with faculty advisor  •  6 July 2026  •  Status: pre-project planning document

The question in one paragraph. AI models that read ECGs (heart-rhythm recordings) are trained mostly on data from a handful of wealthy countries. When these same models are used on patients from other backgrounds, they make more mistakes, especially false alarms: flagging something as disease when it is actually a harmless variation. Nobody in the published literature has actually pinned down why this happens, and the "why" matters, because at least two very different explanations are on the table, and they need completely different fixes. Either the model's ability to read the signal itself breaks down on unfamiliar patterns (a perception problem), or the model reads the signal correctly but has quietly learned to apply a population-based assumption that doesn't hold in the new setting (closer to a reasoning problem than a perception one). It's a bit like a doctor who reads X-rays very well at one hospital and starts making mistakes at a new one: is his eye genuinely worse at the new scans, or is he unconsciously carrying over an old assumption about who tends to get sick? You'd want to know which one it is before trying to fix it.

This document has three parts: what has already been established, with sources (Section 1); the specific, verified gaps in that picture (Section 2); and what we propose to do about each one, with an honest account of why it's genuinely new (Section 3). Section 4 covers a few things that don't fit neatly into "filling a gap" because nobody has framed the question this way at all.

1. What's already been established (verified, top-tier sources only)

Every entry below is a peer-reviewed paper in a recognized top-tier venue, independently checked against the original source, not copied from a secondary summary. Where a source is a preprint or workshop paper rather than a peer-reviewed journal or conference, it is marked clearly, we've kept a few of these in because they're directly relevant, but we're not pretending they carry the same weight as a Nature paper.

1.1  The AI models this research is about

ECGFounder
NEJM AI, 2025
Paper: arxiv.org/abs/2410.04133  |  Code: github.com/PKUDigitalHealth/ECGFounder  |  Weights: huggingface.co/PKUDigitalHealth/ECGFounder
What it is: A supervised foundation model trained on 10,771,552 ECGs from 1,818,247 people (Harvard–Emory database), covering 150 diagnostic labels.
Limitation: Trained on one health system's patient mix; the paper does not investigate what specifically drives errors when applied to a different population.
ECG-FM
JAMIA Open, 2025
Paper: arxiv.org/abs/2408.05178  |  Code: github.com/bowang-lab/ECG-FM
What it is: A fully open-weight foundation model trained on 1.5 million ECGs using contrastive multi-segment coding.
Limitation: Open weights make it usable for the kind of internal-inspection work this project needs, but no one has done that inspection yet.
Cardiac Sensing Foundation Model (CSFM)
Nature Machine Intelligence, Feb 2026
DOI: 10.1038/s42256-026-01180-5  |  Code: github.com/guxiao0822/Cardiac-Sensing-FM
What it is: A transformer trained on ECG, smartwatch PPG, and text from about 1.7 million people, unifying hospital and consumer-device signals.
Limitation: Solves cross-device bias (hospital vs. smartwatch), not cross-population bias.
DeepECG-SSL / DeepECG-SL
European Heart Journal, 2026
DOI: 10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf1119
What it is: A head-to-head test of self-supervised vs. supervised ECG pretraining, externally validated across 11 geographically different cohorts.
Limitation: Measures whether performance holds up across geography, not why it doesn't when it fails.

1.2  The evidence that a real bias problem exists

AMIE (Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer)
Nature Medicine, Feb 2026
DOI: 10.1038/s41591-025-04190-9
What it is: A randomized trial of LLM-assisted cardiology (built on Gemini), reducing clinically significant errors from 24.3% to 13.1% when nine cardiologists were assisted by the system, judged by a blinded 10-domain rubric.
Limitation: Single US center, English-only. The authors themselves note there wasn't enough demographic variation in the sample to test for bias at all, this is the field's current gold-standard evaluation method, applied to a population narrow enough that the question this project asks was never tested.
Race signatures in the ECG are largely environmental, not genetic
Bollepalli et al., npj Cardiovascular Health, 2025
DOI: 10.1038/s44325-025-00087-1
What it is: Across ~10 million ECGs, an AI model reads race from the raw signal at 86% accuracy. The signal is weaker in lower-income patients and shifts with age, both signs pointing toward environmental and social origins rather than fixed biology.
Limitation: Shows that population information is detectable inside an ECG. Does not connect this to diagnostic false positives or propose a fix.
Demographic "shortcuts" cause fairness gaps, and fixes don't transfer across hospitals
Nature Medicine, 2024 (Ghassemi et al. / MIT)
news.mit.edu/2024/study-reveals-why-ai-analyzed-medical-images-can-be-biased-0628
What it is: In chest X-ray models, demographic information acts as a hidden "shortcut" that drives unequal error rates. Critically, a follow-up study found that debiasing a model at one hospital does not reliably transfer when the model is deployed at a different hospital.
Limitation: Imaging only, not ECG. Only training-time fixes were tested.
Cross-ethnic ECG fairness has not been tested, and the field says so in print
DA-GAT-v2, Scientific Reports, June 2026
DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-54206-8
What it is: A fairness-aware ECG model that handles sex and age bias well, but the authors state directly that neither of their datasets carries race or ethnicity information, and that testing on a dataset that does (they name MIMIC-IV-ECG specifically) is required before any cross-ethnic fairness claim can be made.
Limitation: This is, in effect, the field publicly flagging the exact gap this project addresses, and confirming it hasn't been filled yet.
KERALA-AF: scanned paper ECGs carry real diagnostic signal
Lancet Regional Health – Southeast Asia, 2026
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12828833
What it is: In a South Asian atrial fibrillation cohort, a model combining scanned paper ECGs with clinical data predicted stroke risk better (AUC 0.816) than the standard clinical score (AUC 0.666), a permutation analysis showed over half of that predictive power came from the scanned images alone.
Limitation: One registry, 631 patients. Doesn't test foundation models or examine why any model would fail on this population.

1.3  Tools borrowed from other fields (proven elsewhere, never applied here)

Sparse Feature Circuits
Marks, Rager, Michaud, Belinkov, Bau, Mueller — ICLR 2025 (Oral)
arxiv.org/abs/2403.19647
What it is: A method for finding the specific, human-interpretable internal features of a neural network that causally drive a given behavior, and editing them directly.
Limitation: Demonstrated only on language models. Never applied to a physiological signal model of any kind.
LEACE (concept erasure)
Belrose, Schneider-Joseph, Ravfogel, Cotterell, Raff, Biderman — NeurIPS 2023
arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819  |  Code: github.com/EleutherAI/concept-erasure
What it is: A closed-form, mathematically exact method for removing a specific concept's signature from a model's internal representations, without retraining the model.
Limitation: Proven on language models. Never applied to a medical time-series encoder.
Mechanistic interpretability works on physiological signal models too, not just language
Lehn-Schiøler et al., preprint, May 2026 PREPRINT
arxiv.org/abs/2605.13930
What it is: Sparse autoencoders trained on three EEG (brainwave) foundation models genuinely found an internal "age and disease are tangled together" feature, and could steer it directly. This is the proof that the toolkit this project needs works on physiological signals, not just text.
Limitation: EEG only. Never done on ECG. Doesn't look at ancestry or population at all.
Causal decomposition of medical AI performance drops
Gordaliza et al., MedEurIPS 2025 workshop WORKSHOP PAPER
arxiv.org/abs/2512.09094  |  Code: github.com/PeterMcGor/CausalDropMedImg
What it is: Uses game-theory-style attribution (Shapley values) to split an imaging model's performance drop into "different scanner" vs. "different annotator," for MS lesion segmentation.
Limitation: Only two mechanisms, imaging only, does not include population/prevalence differences at all. A workshop paper, not yet through full peer review.

1.4  Supporting data and infrastructure

MIMIC-IV-ECG (linked to MIMIC-IV clinical records)
PhysioNet, credentialed access
physionet.org/content/mimic-iv-ecg
What it is: 795,546 ECGs from 159,608 patients at one US hospital system, with race and ethnicity recorded (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, and finer categories) for every patient.
UK Biobank (ECG substudy + genetic ancestry labels)
Application-based access
ukbiobank.ac.uk
What it is: Around 90,000 participants have a resting 12-lead ECG on file. Separately, genetic ancestry analysis of the cohort has identified 2,782 participants of predominantly South Asian ancestry (plus ~6,650 African and ~2,360 East Asian), all recruited and scanned under the exact same protocol as everyone else.
Note: How many of the South Asian-ancestry participants specifically have an ECG on file is not yet confirmed, this needs to be checked early, since it determines whether that arm of the analysis is adequately powered.
ECG-Image-Kit & ECG-Image-Database
Physiological Measurement, 2024
DOI: 10.1088/1361-6579/ad4954  |  Code: github.com/alphanumericslab/ecg-image-kit  |  Data: arxiv.org/abs/2409.16612
What it is: An open-source toolkit that renders any digital ECG as a realistic scanned paper image (creases, handwriting, phone-photo distortion included), plus a real 35,595-image dataset of actual paper ECGs. Already used in the official 2024 PhysioNet Challenge.

2. The genuine gaps

Each gap below is stated as something the papers in Section 1 collectively prove is missing, not something we're merely assuming.
1Nobody has broken down what the cross-population gap is actually made of.
Evidence: DeepECG-SSL measures whether the gap exists across 11 cohorts but not why. Gordaliza et al. split imaging errors into only two causes (scanner vs. annotator), and explicitly not population. DA-GAT-v2 says outright that the ethnicity-specific analysis hasn't been done.
2No one has looked inside an ECG model to find what's mechanistically driving the errors.
Evidence: the interpretability toolkit (sparse autoencoders, causal feature-editing) is proven on language models and, as of May 2026, on EEG. It has never been pointed at an ECG foundation model.
3Every fix that exists today needs retraining, and retraining-based fixes are known not to travel.
Evidence: the Nature Medicine follow-up to the chest X-ray shortcut work shows debiasing at one hospital fails to transfer to another. No inference-time, label-free correction has been attempted on any medical time-series model.
4Nobody knows if ECG failure is even the same kind of problem as imaging failure.
Evidence: the "demographic shortcut" story is established for chest X-rays specifically. Whether the same mechanism, versus a genuinely different one, explains ECG failure has never been tested.
5No existing ECG study can tell ancestry apart from acquisition (device, hospital, site).
Evidence: every cross-country ECG comparison in the literature changes the hospital, the devices, and the population all at once, so a measured gap could be caused by any of them. Nobody has used a within-hospital, multi-ancestry design to isolate the ancestry effect specifically.

3. What we're proposing, gap by gap

1&5Decompose the gap properly, using a design that finally isolates ancestry
What we'll do
Instead of comparing countries, compare ancestry groups within the same hospital system: MIMIC-IV-ECG (US hospital, same devices, patients recorded as White/Black/Hispanic/Asian) and UK Biobank (same protocol, includes a labeled South Asian group). Since the hospital and equipment stay fixed, any remaining gap can only be coming from the patient's background, not the machine. We then split that gap into five separate pieces mathematically: different disease rates, different equipment/format, harmless look-alike variation, genuinely different disease presentation, and different labeling standards, using a fair statistical attribution method (a Shapley decomposition) that credits each cause its fair share.
Why this is genuinely new
The "same hospital, different ancestry" trick has never been used for this question in ECG. The five-way split has never been done at all, anywhere, for any medical signal, the closest existing version only splits two causes, for imaging.
Verified by: searching specifically for cross-ancestry, within-site ECG decomposition studies and finding none; confirming MIMIC-IV-ECG's race/ethnicity coverage and UK Biobank's South Asian ancestry group directly from primary sources.
2&4Open the model up and find the actual mechanism
What we'll do
Train sparse autoencoders (a technique that untangles a model's internal activity into individually meaningful pieces) on open-weight models like ECG-FM and ECGFounder. Look for a specific internal signal tied to the patient's ancestry, then test, by manually injecting that signal from one ECG into another and watching what the model does, whether it actually causes the wrong diagnosis, rather than just being associated with it. If we find such a signal, we get a cardiologist to identify what it physically corresponds to on the waveform.
Why this is genuinely new
This exact toolkit has been proven to work on EEG as of a few months ago. It has never been tried on ECG. Nobody has tested, causally rather than just statistically, whether ECG failure works the same way imaging failure does, or is something else entirely, both possible answers would be new findings.
Verified by: confirming the EEG precedent directly, and confirming no published or preprint work applies sparse autoencoders to any ECG foundation model as of this writing.
3Try to fix it without retraining, and without needing labels from the new population
What we'll do
If Gap 2's work finds a real internal pathway, use a mathematically exact method (LEACE) to surgically delete just that pathway from the model's internals, at the moment of use, with the model's weights left completely untouched. Check carefully that this doesn't also delete anything genuinely diagnostic. Compare directly against the existing fixes (retraining, reweighting) on both fairness and accuracy.
Why this is genuinely new
This exact tool has only ever been used on language models. If it works here, it would be the first correction of its kind, one that could in principle be deployed in a low-resource clinic without any local retraining or new labeled data at all, which existing fixes all require.
Verified by: confirming LEACE has no prior application to any medical signal model, and that all existing ECG fairness work (DA-GAT-v2, the demographic-suppression VAE approach) requires retraining.

4. Beyond the gaps: things that have never been framed this way at all

These aren't gaps in someone else's work, they're places nobody has asked the question in the first place.
A pre-registered design where every possible result is a real finding

We are writing down, before running a single experiment, exactly what conclusion each possible outcome leads to, and filing it as a timestamped pre-registration. If the mechanism is found and the fix works, that's a flagship result. If the mechanism is found but the fix has a real accuracy cost, that's a finding about a fundamental limit. If the mechanism isn't there at all, that's a finding that ECG doesn't behave like imaging does, itself worth knowing. If nothing works without new labeled data, that's a strong, field-relevant negative result about the limits of what post-hoc correction can do. There is no version of this where we end up with nothing to publish, and that structure itself, designing a study this way for a bias question in physiological AI, is not something we found anywhere else in the literature either.

A cross-population claim that needs zero clinical data collaborations

Because the entire design runs on MIMIC-IV, UK Biobank, and other fully public datasets, the core scientific claim doesn't depend on securing a hospital partnership anywhere. That also means the finding, if it holds, is a general property of how these models represent patient information, not a fact about any single country's data, which is precisely what distinguishes a global scientific contribution from a local audit.

5. Read this before presenting it

No one, including us, can promise in advance that this will land in Nature. What we can stand behind, in writing, is this: every claim of "this hasn't been done" above was checked against the actual published record, not assumed, and every tool we're proposing to borrow has a verified track record somewhere else. That combination, an honestly mapped gap plus a pre-registered design where any outcome is worth publishing, is what actually earns a strong shot at a top journal. It's also exactly the standard your advisor has been holding this project to from the start.
